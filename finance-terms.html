The Investor's Glossary

Finance bro terms, decoded.

The acronyms get thrown around like everyone's supposed to already know them. Here's what they actually mean, with the formulas and why they matter, in plain English.

P/EEPSEBITDAWACC ROEROICMarket CapDiv Yield FCFD/EMarginsBeta
P/EPrice to Earnings
How much you pay for each dollar of a company's profit. A P/E of 20 means you're paying $20 for every $1 the company earns per year.
Formula
P/E = Share Price ÷ Earnings Per Share
Why it matters: a high P/E can mean a stock is expensive, or that investors expect strong growth. A low P/E can mean it's cheap, or that growth is expected to slow.
EPSEarnings Per Share
The company's total profit divided across every share. It tells you how much profit each single share earned.
Formula
EPS = Net Profit ÷ Shares Outstanding
Why it matters: EPS growth over time is a sign a company is becoming more profitable. It feeds directly into the P/E ratio.
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortisation
A measure of a company's core operating profit, stripping out financing and accounting effects. It shows how much the actual business makes before the extras.
Formula
EBITDA = Net Profit + Interest + Tax + Depreciation + Amortisation
Why it matters: it lets you compare the operating performance of companies regardless of debt levels or tax situations. Critics say it can hide real costs.
WACC
Weighted Average Cost of Capital
The average rate a company pays to finance itself, blending the cost of its debt and equity. Essentially the minimum return a company needs to be worth investing in.
Formula
WACC = (E/V × Re) + (D/V × Rd × (1−Tax))
Why it matters: if a company can't earn more than its WACC, it's destroying value. Analysts use it as the "discount rate" when valuing companies.
ROEReturn on Equity
How efficiently a company turns shareholder money into profit. An ROE of 15% means it generates $0.15 of profit for every $1 of equity.
Formula
ROE = Net Profit ÷ Shareholder Equity
Why it matters: consistently high ROE often signals a quality business. But high debt can inflate it artificially, so check alongside debt levels.
ROICReturn on Invested Capital
How well a company uses all its capital (debt and equity) to generate returns. A stricter cousin of ROE.
Formula
ROIC = Net Operating Profit After Tax ÷ Invested Capital
Why it matters: when ROIC is higher than WACC, the company is creating value. It's one of the cleanest measures of business quality.
Market Cap
Market Capitalisation
The total value of all a company's shares. It's what the market thinks the whole company is worth right now.
Formula
Market Cap = Share Price × Shares Outstanding
Why it matters: it defines company size. Large-cap (over $10B) tends to be more stable; small-cap can be more volatile but higher growth.
Div Yield
Dividend Yield
The annual dividend as a percentage of the share price. A 4% yield means you get $4 a year in dividends for every $100 invested.
Formula
Dividend Yield = Annual Dividend ÷ Share Price × 100
Why it matters: it shows income return separate from price growth. A very high yield can sometimes be a warning sign of a falling price.
FCFFree Cash Flow
The cash left over after a company pays its running costs and reinvests in itself. Real, spendable cash, not accounting profit.
Formula
FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure
Why it matters: cash is harder to fake than profit. Strong FCF means a company can pay dividends, reduce debt, or reinvest without borrowing.
D/EDebt to Equity
How much debt a company uses compared to shareholder money. A D/E of 1.0 means equal debt and equity.
Formula
D/E = Total Debt ÷ Shareholder Equity
Why it matters: high debt amplifies both gains and risk. What counts as "high" varies hugely by industry, so compare within a sector.
Margins
Gross / Net Profit Margin
The percentage of revenue a company keeps as profit. Gross margin is after direct costs; net margin is after everything.
Formula
Net Margin = Net Profit ÷ Revenue × 100
Why it matters: higher margins mean more profit kept from each sale, often a sign of pricing power or efficiency. Compare within the same industry.
BetaVolatility vs Market
How much a stock moves relative to the overall market. Beta of 1 moves with the market; above 1 is more volatile, below 1 is calmer.
Reading it
Beta = 1.5 → 50% more volatile than market
Beta = 0.5 → half as volatile
Why it matters: it's a quick gauge of risk. High-beta stocks swing harder in both directions; low-beta stocks are steadier.
General information only. These definitions are educational and simplified. They are not financial product advice and do not consider your personal circumstances. Financial metrics should never be used in isolation. Always do your own research and consider professional advice before making investment decisions.
